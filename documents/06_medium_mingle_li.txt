I made plenty of mistakes, and only through making these mistakes did I learn what to do differently for the next co-op search. I’m writing this to share what I did wrong, what I should’ve done, and I hope you can take away something from this to help you on your co-op search! Best of luck!   1. Let the employer establish details first!    What I Did Wrong:  When I asked an employer “what is your compensation / pay for this co-op position”, they replied “what are you looking for?” I answered with a pay I thought was reasonable, a bit on the higher side for the company.   What I Should’ve Done:  I should’ve said “I am not comfortable answering that question until I know more about the base compensation for this position”. Let the ball start on their playing field when it comes to the pay! If you establish a pay that’s “what you’re looking for”, and it’s not something they can provide, it gets awkward — fast.   2. Money isn’t everything!    What I Did Wrong:  Going into the co-op search (and going into Northeastern), I was hoping that the co-op pay would help pay for my tuition cost. Thus, I put a lot of emphasis — too much — on the pay. The average is $26/hr, and I didn’t want to settle for anything below $30/hr.   What I Should’ve Done:  The point of the first-time co-op is  experience , not the money.  The money will come with experience and time!  Do not stress about getting the highest pay. As you continue on future co-op searches, employers will pay more to reflect the co-op experience you’ve earned.   3. Again: the experience is what matters!    What I Did Wrong:  I did not truly understand or value the experience that a co-op would bring. If you have to decide between a co-op that does fully hands-on software engineering for $20/hr, and a co-op where you’d barely write any code for $40/hr, choose the first one. Because I focused on money too much, I probably would’ve chosen the second co-op.   What I Should’ve Done:  The most important thing to remember is that this first co-op, and co-ops in general, isn’t about your present.  It’s about your future.  The more experience and the more things you can really  do  in your co-op, the more substantial content you can put on your resume, and the better you look for future employers once you’re out of college and entering the workforce. Make the most of your co-op by prioritizing experience and what you’re going to get out of it.   4. Do NOT bring self-worth into this co-op search!    What I Did Wrong : Entering this co-op search, I thought highly of myself and said to myself, “because of my past experience, I’m worth a lot to employers! I should be paid higher than average!”   What I Should’ve Done:  Leave self-worth behind, because even though you have a lot of value, these companies view you as  a first-time co-op student with 0 co-op experience . Don’t take it personally if results aren’t what you hoped for!   5. There’s no need to “prove yourself” on NUWorks!    What I Did Wrong:  I thought that in order to “prove myself” that I was “good enough” to get a co-op “legitimately” through NUWorks, I had to go through the grueling process of cold-dropping my resume everywhere and doing the online assessment, then behavioral interview, then technical interview pathway.   What I Should’ve Done:  This was more of a personal mental block than anything. Any co-op that you get is “legitimate”. Doing the NUWorks search is equivalent to playing the lottery (especially in this job economy) and it says nothing about your self-worth or you being “good enough”.   6. Use your connections!    What I Did Wrong:  Because I thought I needed to go the “legitimate” NUWorks way, I didn’t use my connections and contacts until late into the co-op search after, surprise, the NUWorks way didn’t turn out great.   What I Should’ve Done:  Contact your connections — contact them now, even. People that you’ve worked with, people that are connected to your family or parents somehow, etc. Someone could give you a  referral , and let me emphasize this:  a referral carries a LOT of weight . Like, an astounding amount of weight, because it makes you stand out from the hundreds of applicants. Don’t be afraid to contact your connections and ask them to help you out.   Personal Tidbit:  The co-op I found is not through NUWorks. It is through a high school internship connection. I’m currently applying to a company for a summer internship, and the only reason I got so far is because I contacted my dad’s old boss from a decade ago and he gave me a referral, which accelerated me to the head of the line of applicants. I tried applying to that same company during the co-op search, and I got ghosted/rejected. TL;DR:  A referral will boost you big time!    7. Do projects!    What I Did Wrong:  I don’t think I did anything wrong here, I have some good projects on my resume and I thought of this point because I realized how important it is for co-ops.   What You Should Do:  This is important because you need something to talk about with employers, something you’ve done that’s  special  — and no, class projects do not count. An employer even stated that they’ve seen “Image Processor” thousands of times on students’ resumes. Your projects can be small, but it should show that you learned something new!   Some Suggestions:  We learn Java at Northeastern, but lots of co-op listings are software engineering and many places use JavaScript front-end frameworks like React, Angular, and Vue.  I really recommend React  and would suggest learning it and creating something cool, and you can put it as a project on your resume!   8. Practice Leetcode Problem Solving!   What I Did Wrong:  I did not use the summer before this semester to practice Leetcode / algorithmic problems. Thus, I spent this semester doing OOD, Algo, Co-op search, AND practicing Leetcode.   What I Should’ve Done:  I should’ve started practicing earlier.  Get comfortable with Leetcode Easy’s , or the equivalent. The point is here is that you need a general knowledge of data structures (e.g. HashMap, stack, queue, linked list), strategies to use (e.g. two-pointer, sorting, binary search), and  the ability to problem solve .   9. Apply super early!    What I Did Wrong:  Employers do not wait until the “due date” of a listing is over, and then look through all applicants. They review applicants on a first-come, first-serve basis. I didn’t know this and I took my time to craft a cover letter, only for the job listing to be pulled early when I came back.   What I Should’ve Done:  I should’ve had a cover letter template made already (more below), and I should’ve applied the instant the job listing came out.  Monitor NUWorks daily!  Do not wait; if you are the first applicant to the job listing, you will be the first to be seen and reviewed.   10. Craft a cover letter template!    What I Did Wrong:  I made a cover letter template too late and so I took too much time writing a cover letter, so I was late to apply to the job listing.   What I Should’ve Done:  I should’ve created a cover letter template in the beginning of the co-op search, to make creating cover letters quicker and easier.   Some Advice… this winter break,   Create a general cover letter template, do a project or two, and practice algorithmic questions!   Result of NUWorks Co-op Search:   Remember that after applying to these 100 listings, I ended up getting my co-op through a personal connection instead of applying through NUWorks. You can see that the odds are tough in the co-op search, but it’ll work out in the end!            Also just a note:  Most of the co-ops listed on NUWorks are in Massachusetts. Just a heads up: in case you were shooting to be in a specific location for your co-op, the chance that there will be many listings in that location is not that high.   Thank you for reading!   I hope these tips will help you succeed in finding a co-op! Feel free to bookmark / save this doc to refer to later in the year. Best of luck, and feel free to contact me with any questions or concerns!
